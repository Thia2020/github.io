Cyber Resilience Toolkit

Cyber Resilience Toolkit for Small Business

A practical cybersecurity toolkit designed to help small businesses improve cyber resilience through straightforward governance, identity protection, backup planning, incident readiness, and business-focused security practices.

Overview

Why this toolkit was created

Many small businesses do not have dedicated cybersecurity staff but still face significant risks from phishing, ransomware, credential theft, and accidental data loss. This toolkit was developed to provide practical, low-cost measures that improve operational resilience and reduce cyber risk.

Practical Toolkit

Cyber resilience tools and checklists

These practical tools are designed to help small businesses assess risk, improve readiness, and make sensible cybersecurity improvements without unnecessary complexity.

1. Cyber Health Check

  • Multi-factor authentication is enabled for email and key business systems.
  • Business email accounts are protected with strong, unique passwords.
  • Administrator accounts are limited and separated from everyday user accounts.
  • Endpoint protection is installed and active on business devices.
  • Operating systems, browsers, and applications are kept up to date.
  • Important business data is backed up and recovery has been tested.
  • Staff know how to recognise and report phishing attempts.

2. Cyber Risk Register

A simple register for recording cyber risks, ownership, likelihood, impact, treatment actions, and review status.

  • Identify key cyber risks such as phishing, ransomware, data loss, or account compromise.
  • Assess likelihood and business impact.
  • Assign an owner for each risk.
  • Document treatment actions and due dates.
  • Review risks regularly as the business changes.

3. Incident Response Card

If a cyber incident is suspected, the business should:

  1. Disconnect affected devices from the network where safe to do so.
  2. Do not delete files, wipe systems, or make unnecessary changes.
  3. Record what happened, when it was noticed, and who was involved.
  4. Preserve suspicious emails, files, screenshots, and system messages.
  5. Contact the nominated IT or cybersecurity support person.
  6. Consider legal, privacy, customer, or regulatory notification obligations.

4. Backup & Recovery Checklist

  • Critical business data and systems have been identified.
  • Backups are performed regularly and stored securely.
  • At least one backup is protected from ransomware or accidental deletion.
  • Restore testing is performed periodically.
  • Recovery contacts and responsibilities are documented.
  • Business continuity steps are understood if systems are unavailable.

5. Identity Protection Checklist

  • MFA is enabled for Microsoft 365, banking, accounting, and administration accounts.
  • Shared passwords are avoided wherever possible.
  • Password managers are used for strong and unique passwords.
  • Departed staff and unused accounts are promptly disabled.
  • Access permissions are reviewed regularly.
  • Privileged access is limited to people who genuinely need it.

6. Vendor Security Checklist

  • Does the vendor support multi-factor authentication?
  • Where is business or customer data stored?
  • How does the vendor protect backups?
  • What happens if the vendor suffers a cyber incident?
  • Does the vendor provide clear privacy and security information?
  • Can access be removed quickly if the service is no longer used?

7. Executive Cyber Summary

A one-page summary to help business owners understand their current cyber posture, key risks, priority actions, and practical next steps.

  • Current cyber posture: Low, moderate, or high concern.
  • Top three cyber risks affecting the business.
  • Controls already in place.
  • Priority actions for the next 30, 60, and 90 days.
  • Items requiring owner or management decision.
