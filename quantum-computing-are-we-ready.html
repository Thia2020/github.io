Quantum Computing: Are We Ready for What Comes Next?

By Cynthia Wilkinson | Thia-W Computing Solutions  •  15 November 2026

There is another technology developing that could change the world.

It isn't artificial intelligence.

It's quantum computing.

Quantum computing could help scientists develop new medicines, discover new materials and solve complex optimisation problems. Australia sees quantum technology as an important scientific and economic opportunity.

But there is another side to this story.

The same technology that could help solve problems today's computers struggle with may eventually undermine some of the cryptography we depend upon to protect our information.

Does this mean quantum computers are about to break the internet?

No.

But governments, cybersecurity agencies, researchers and technology companies are already preparing for what could happen in the future.

Sometimes cyber resilience isn't about responding to something that has already happened. Sometimes it's about seeing change coming and preparing before it arrives.

The Promise of Quantum Computing

Quantum computing is not inherently a threat. Its potential benefits are extraordinary.

The Australian Government's National Quantum Strategy identifies opportunities across quantum computing, communications and sensing.

Potential applications include medicine, materials science, transport, supply chains, mineral exploration and complex optimisation.

Quantum computers approach certain computational problems differently from the classical computers we use today.

Classical computers process information using bits, represented as either zero or one.

Quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, which exploit properties of quantum mechanics, including superposition, interference and entanglement.

This does not mean quantum computers will replace every computer we use today. They are being developed for particular kinds of problems, and classical and quantum computing are expected to work together.

Where Are We Today?

Quantum computers already exist, but they are still developing. Building sufficiently large, reliable, error-corrected quantum computers remains an enormous technical challenge.

One important term in cybersecurity is a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC).

This refers to a quantum computer capable of threatening the cryptographic systems we rely upon today.

As at September 2026, when I recorded my video on this subject, such a computer did not exist. The timing of its arrival remains uncertain.

Why Cybersecurity Professionals Are Paying Attention

Cryptography is fundamental to modern cybersecurity.

It helps protect communications, authenticate systems, verify digital signatures and safeguard information.

Some widely used public-key cryptographic systems depend on mathematical problems that are extremely difficult for classical computers to solve.

In 1994, mathematician Peter Shor developed an algorithm showing how a sufficiently capable quantum computer could solve certain mathematical problems efficiently.

This has implications for widely used public-key systems such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography.

The concern is not that current quantum computers can routinely defeat these systems.

The concern is that changing cryptography across governments, businesses, applications, cloud platforms, networks and critical infrastructure could take many years.

Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

There is another reason organisations cannot necessarily wait until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists.

It is known as harvest now, decrypt later.

Imagine an attacker steals encrypted information today but cannot read it.

Instead of discarding the information, the attacker keeps it.

Years later, if quantum computing reaches the point where the cryptography protecting that information can be defeated, the attacker may be able to decrypt the stored copy.

This is particularly important for information that must remain confidential for many years.

  • Sensitive government information
  • Intellectual property and commercial secrets
  • Long-term confidential health information
  • Identity information
  • Other information with lasting confidentiality requirements

The risk depends on the cryptography used and the information exposed. Not every encrypted system is equally vulnerable to quantum attacks.

For some organisations, the need to prepare begins well before a capable quantum computer becomes available.

Post-Quantum Cryptography

Fortunately, researchers have not been waiting for the threat to materialise.

Post-quantum cryptography, or PQC, refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to resist attacks from both classical and quantum computers.

In 2024, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) finalised its first three major post-quantum cryptography standards:

  • ML-KEM — for establishing shared encryption keys.
  • ML-DSA — for digital signatures.
  • SLH-DSA — another digital signature algorithm.

These standards represent an important step towards protecting systems against future quantum threats.

The transition to post-quantum cryptography is already underway.

Australia Is Preparing for the Transition

The Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) has published guidance and transition milestones for moving away from vulnerable traditional asymmetric cryptography.

The milestones outlined in the guidance discussed in my September 2026 video were:

By the end of 2026

Organisations should have a refined plan for transitioning to post-quantum cryptography.

By the end of 2028

Organisations should have commenced the transition, prioritising critical systems and data.

By the end of 2030

Organisations should have completed their transition in accordance with ASD's guidance.

These milestones illustrate why organisations need to begin planning before the anticipated technological threat becomes a reality.

Organisations must identify where cryptography is used, understand what it protects, assess critical systems, engage suppliers, plan upgrades and test changes.

ASD's LATICE framework provides a structured approach:

  • Locate
  • Assess
  • Triage
  • Implement
  • Communicate and educate

To me, this is a practical example of cyber resilience.

Does This Remind You of Y2K?

For those who remember the Year 2000 problem, there is something familiar about this transition.

The technical problems are entirely different. Y2K concerned the representation of dates in computer systems.

But the organisational challenge has similarities.

Businesses had to identify vulnerable systems, understand dependencies, contact suppliers, update technology, test changes and prepare contingency plans.

When 1 January 2000 arrived, widespread disruption was largely avoided.

One reason was the extensive preparation undertaken beforehand.

Successful resilience can sometimes look like nothing happened.

But there is an important difference.

Y2K had a known deadline.

With quantum computing, we do not know when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will become available.

How do we encourage organisations to prepare when we cannot tell them exactly when the threat will materialise?

What Should Small Businesses Do?

Small businesses do not need to hire quantum physicists.

Much of the technical transition may happen through the technology providers they already depend upon.

Operating systems, browsers, cloud platforms, business applications, security products and managed service providers will play important roles in this transition.

But that does not mean small businesses have nothing to do.

They should begin by understanding their technology dependencies and asking practical questions.

  • Which systems and services are critical to our business?
  • What information must remain confidential for many years?
  • Which technology suppliers do we depend upon?
  • Are our products and systems still supported?
  • Are our vendors preparing for post-quantum cryptography?
  • Do we have older systems that may be difficult to upgrade?
  • Can we accommodate significant security upgrades without disrupting operations?

These questions are not unique to quantum computing. They are part of good technology governance and cyber resilience.

Why Crypto-Agility Matters

Another important concept is crypto-agility.

Crypto-agility is the ability to change cryptographic mechanisms as requirements, standards and threats evolve, without unnecessarily rebuilding entire systems.

This matters beyond quantum computing.

Cryptographic algorithms can become outdated, vulnerabilities can be discovered and standards can change.

Rather than assuming a cryptographic mechanism will remain suitable forever, organisations should consider how they will adapt when changes become necessary.

If the cryptography we depend upon has to change, can we change with it?

What I Anticipated in September 2026

When I recorded my video in September 2026, I deliberately avoided predicting when quantum computing would transform cybersecurity.

Instead, I identified developments worth watching.

  • Post-quantum cryptography becoming increasingly common in everyday technology products.
  • Organisations gaining better visibility of where cryptography is used.
  • Vendor readiness becoming part of cyber-risk and technology-management discussions.
  • Greater attention to information requiring long-term confidentiality.
  • Crypto-agility becoming an increasingly important organisational capability.
  • Quantum readiness becoming part of broader cyber resilience planning.

I am also interested in how artificial intelligence and quantum computing may eventually develop together.

Researchers are exploring the possibilities, but it would be premature to make confident predictions about their combined impact on cybersecurity.

These are developments to monitor, not outcomes we can assume.

Why This Is a Cyber Resilience Story

There has been no quantum cyber catastrophe for us to analyse.

There is no breach report or incident reconstruction at the centre of this story.

Instead, we are looking forward.

Cyber resilience is often described in terms of responding to incidents, recovering and learning.

But resilience also involves identifying emerging risks, understanding their implications, preparing, monitoring developments and adapting as the evidence changes.

We do not have to wait for something to fail before becoming more resilient.

The Lesson

Quantum computing could become one of the most important technological developments of our time.

Its potential benefits are considerable, but technological progress can also change cybersecurity risk.

Fortunately, post-quantum standards already exist, governments are planning transitions, and technology companies are beginning to adapt.

Organisations can begin by understanding their critical information, technology dependencies and capacity to change.

We do not know exactly when the future we are preparing for will arrive.

Cyber resilience isn't about predicting the future perfectly. It's about anticipating change, preparing for it, learning as the evidence evolves, and adapting.

The question is: will we be ready?
